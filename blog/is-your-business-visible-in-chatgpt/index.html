Is your business visible in ChatGPT?

Petr BelyaevNovember 24, 20267 min read

More and more buyers start with a question to an AI assistant instead of a search box: “which agency can help me get residency in Greece”, “best tour operator for a northern lights trip”, “who sets up ClickUp for a small agency”. The assistant answers with a few names. If yours is not among them, you never hear about that buyer.

Most business owners I talk to have no idea whether they are in those answers. Some have asked ChatGPT once, saw their name or didn’t, and drew a conclusion. That is not a measurement. Here is how I measure it for clients, and what the numbers can and cannot tell you.

Step 1. Ask the questions your buyers ask — without your name

The most common mistake is asking “what do you know about [my company]?”. The assistant will happily tell you, and it proves nothing. Buyers who don’t know you yet don’t type your name.

So the first step is a list of real buyer questions about your niche, in the languages of your market. Usually 30 to 100 of them is enough to see a pattern. Good sources are your sales calls, the questions your AI agent or managers get most often, and the queries your SEO or content contractor already works with.

A useful question sounds like a buyer, not like a marketer: “how long does it take to get a residence permit in Portugal”, not “top immigration services Portugal 2026”.

Step 2. Ask more than one assistant, more than once

ChatGPT, Claude and Perplexity give different answers to the same question, and each of them gives different answers on different runs. One run is a snapshot, not a fact.

That’s why I ask every question to each assistant several times per run, with web search turned on, and count the share of answers that mention the brand. “Mentioned in 14% of answers” is a number you can track. “ChatGPT mentioned us yesterday” is an anecdote.

Step 3. Read the answers like a competitor analyst

For each answer I record four things:

  1. Is the brand mentioned — by any of its names, spellings or domains. Brands are written in surprisingly many ways, so I keep a small dictionary of variants for each client; without it, part of the mentions gets lost.
  2. Is the website linked as a source.
  3. The position among the companies named.
  4. Who is named instead. This is often the most valuable part: the list of competitors that assistants recommend for your buyers’ questions, and the sources they cite for them.

Step 4. Get a baseline before anyone “optimizes”

If you are about to pay for GEO (generative engine optimization) — content and changes meant to get you into AI answers — measure first. Otherwise, in three months you will have the contractor’s report and nothing to compare it with.

Baselines are often sobering. In one baseline I measured for a client in a competitive service niche, the brand appeared in 1 answer out of 92 to questions that didn’t include its name. That is not a disaster; it’s a starting point that makes every later change visible.

What the numbers can’t tell you

I’d rather say this upfront than have a client discover it later:

  • API answers are close to, but not the same as, what a person sees. I query the assistants through their APIs, without a user’s chat history, location or personalization. That makes runs repeatable — which is what you need to track change — but one person’s screen can differ.
  • Google’s AI Overviews are a separate story. They can’t be queried the same way and need a paid search-data service. If Google’s AI answers matter in your market, that is a separate, priced addition.
  • Visibility is not sales. Being mentioned more often is a leading indicator. Whether it brings buyers is answered by your CRM: ask new leads where they heard about you and add “AI assistant” as an option.

What to do with the result

If the share of mentions is low, the usual levers are the same ones that help regular search, with a few additions:

  • Pages that answer the exact questions buyers ask, in plain language, one question per section.
  • Clear facts that are easy to quote: who you serve, where, for how much, how long it takes.
  • Structured data (schema.org) and a simple llms.txt file that describes your site for AI tools.
  • Presence in the sources assistants already cite for your niche — the competitor list from Step 3 shows where those are.

Then you run the same questions again next month and see whether the number moved.

Can you do this yourself?

Yes, with a spreadsheet and patience: 50 questions, three assistants, three repeats is 450 answers to read every month. That’s exactly why I automated it — an n8n workflow sends the questions on a schedule, parses the answers, writes the history to a Google Sheet and sends a short summary to Telegram.
