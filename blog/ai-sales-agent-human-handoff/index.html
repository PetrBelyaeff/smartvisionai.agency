When an AI sales agent should hand over to a person

Petr BelyaevNovember 24, 20266 min read

When a business owner imagines an AI sales agent, they imagine the conversation: fast, polite, always online. When I build one, most of my attention goes to a different moment — the second the agent stops and a person takes over. That handoff is where agents win or lose deals.

A badly designed handoff looks like this: the client has spent ten minutes explaining their situation to the agent, a manager appears, and the first thing they write is “Hello! How can I help you?”. The client has to repeat everything. Some don’t.

Here is how I design the handoff in the agents I build.

1. Decide what the agent is for — and what it isn’t

Before writing a single instruction, I agree with the owner on the agent’s job. Usually it is: answer the first questions, qualify, and bring a warm lead to a call or a manager. It is almost never “close the deal on its own”.

That decision defines the handoff. If the agent’s job ends at a qualified lead, then a qualified lead is the trigger — not a failure of the agent.

2. Write explicit triggers

“Hand over when needed” is not an instruction. The agent needs concrete triggers. The ones I use most often:

  • The lead is qualified: the agent has the answers your managers need (for example budget, timeline, destination, family size) and the client agreed to the next step.
  • The client asks for a person — in any wording. Never argue with this.
  • Money or commitments: discounts outside the price list, special payment terms, guarantees, legal questions. The agent doesn’t improvise here.
  • The answer isn’t in the knowledge base. Guessing is worse than “let me connect you with a colleague who knows this exactly”.
  • Emotion: a complaint, frustration, an existing client with a problem.

3. Hand over the context, not just the chat

The manager should be able to continue the conversation without asking the client to repeat anything. So at the moment of handoff the agent does three things:

  1. Fills the CRM fields — not a wall of text in a note, but the specific fields your pipeline uses: what the client wants, key parameters, what they were offered.
  2. Writes a short summary for the manager: who this is, what they want, what’s been answered, what’s open.
  3. Moves the deal to the right stage and assigns it to the right manager, so it shows up in their work queue — not just in a chat nobody is watching.

4. Tell the client what happens next

From the client’s side, the handoff should feel like being passed to a colleague, not like being dropped. The agent says who will reply and roughly when — and that promise has to be true. If managers don’t work at night, the agent should not say “a manager will reply in five minutes” at 2 a.m.

A handoff message that works: “I’ve passed your details to Anna, our specialist on this program. She’ll message you here tomorrow morning — you won’t need to repeat anything.”

5. Make sure a person actually picks it up

The most painful failures I’ve seen were not bad AI answers. They were handoffs that went nowhere: a notification to a chat that nobody reads, a deal assigned to a manager on vacation, a technical step that silently failed while reporting success.

So the handoff gets the same treatment as any production integration:

  • Routing rules that know who is working, including vacations and days off.
  • An alert if a handed-over lead hasn’t been picked up within an agreed time.
  • Monitoring on the real outcome — the deal is in the manager’s queue — not on “request sent”.

6. Review handoffs every week at first

For the first weeks after launch I read the conversations where the agent handed over, and the ones where it didn’t but probably should have. Most fixes are small: a missing trigger, a CRM field that wasn’t filled, a message that sounded too robotic. Each change to the agent’s instructions goes into a change log, so if something odd happens later, it can be traced to a specific edit.

The short version

An AI agent doesn’t replace your sales team; it changes where your team starts the conversation. The better the handoff, the later — and warmer — that start is.
